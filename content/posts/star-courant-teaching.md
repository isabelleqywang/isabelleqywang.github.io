In spring 2026 I was a teaching assistant at NYU's Courant Institute for Intro to CS and Data Structures and Algorithms. I thought I was signing up to explain things. I ended up learning them properly.

## Situation

Two large courses, more than 200 students between them, and a wide range of starting points. Some students had been coding since high school; others were meeting a pointer for the first time and were not thrilled about it.

## Task

Run weekly office hours that were actually useful, and review student code with feedback that would change how they write the next assignment, not just fix this one.

## Action

In office hours I stopped answering "how do I do this problem" directly and started asking what the data looked like before and after each step. Arrays, recursion, linked lists and binary search trees all became much more approachable once a student drew the state on paper. For recursion in particular, I had people trace the call stack by hand until the base case stopped feeling like magic.

For code review, I focused on three things in order: correctness, then time and space complexity, then readability. I tried to leave one concrete suggestion per issue rather than a general "this could be more efficient", because vague feedback is easy to ignore.

## Result

Across the semester I supported more than 200 students through office hours and reviewed over 20 students' submissions in depth. The moments I remember are the ones where a concept visibly clicked, which is a better metric than anything I could put on a slide.

## Looking back

Explaining pointer manipulation five different ways in one afternoon is lowkey the best interview prep there is. If you cannot explain an algorithm to someone seeing it for the first time, you do not understand it as well as you think, and I found a few of my own gaps that way.

The skill I'm keeping: asking a question that moves someone one step forward instead of handing them the answer. It works on code reviews with colleagues too.
